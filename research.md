# Research

My research focuses on health, development, education, and human capital, with an emphasis on causal evidence from large-scale administrative and survey datasets.

    <p class="research-title">Does Greater Flexibility of Online Labor Markets Encourage Female Labor Force Participation?</p>
    <p class="research-authors">With Rakesh Banerjee, Tushar Bharati, Adnan Fakir, and Yiwei Qian.</p>
    <p class="research-venue">Labor Economics, 2025.</p>
    <div class="paper-actions">
      <a class="paper-button" href="/Published%20Papers/2025,%20Banerjee%20et%20al%20(Labor%20Economics)%20Published%20Version.pdf">Published version</a>
      <details class="abstract-toggle"><summary>Abstract</summary><p>We conducted an experiment on a major international online freelancing platform to examine how increased flexibility in daily work hours affects female participation. We post identical job advertisements covering a wide range of tasks that differ only in flexibility and the wage offered. While both men and women prefer flexibility, the response elasticity for women is twice that for men. Flexible jobs attracted more women and men without compromising application quality, with suggestive evidence that flexible jobs attracted higher-quality female candidates.</p></details>

    <p class="research-title">The Education Gradients in Adult Female Obesity: Causal Evidence from Two African Countries</p>
    <p class="research-authors">With Averi Chakrabarti.</p>
    <p class="research-venue">Journal of Development Effectiveness, 2025.</p>
    <div class="paper-actions">
      <a class="paper-button" href="https://doi.org/10.1080/19439342.2025.2582039">Published version</a>
      <details class="abstract-toggle"><summary>Abstract</summary><p>Obesity rates are rising around the world and so are its health and socioeconomic costs. This paper studies education gradients in adult female obesity in Uganda and Zimbabwe, using age-specific exposure to national school reforms as instruments for women's education. Although cross-sectional associations between education and obesity are positive and significant, the analysis cannot reject the null hypothesis that schooling has no overall causal impact on obesity, body mass index, or being overweight or obese in either setting.</p></details>

  <article class="research-item">
    <p class="research-title">Crime and Human Capital in India</p>
    <p class="research-authors">With Smriti Sharma.</p>
    <p class="research-venue">World Development, 2025.</p>
    <div class="paper-actions">
      <a class="paper-button" href="/Published%20Papers/2025,%20Sharma%20%26%20Sunder%20-%20Crime%20and%20Human%20Capital%20In%20India%20(World%20Dev).pdf">Published version</a>
      <details class="abstract-toggle"><summary>Abstract</summary><p>Violent crime profoundly affects several socioeconomic outcomes. This paper asks whether day-to-day crime also shapes human capital accumulation by combining district-level crime data in India with representative survey data on learning achievement. Increases in violent crime are associated with lower reading and math achievement, while non-violent crimes have no discernible correlation. The associations are stronger for children from socioeconomically disadvantaged households, with evidence pointing to household- and school-level mechanisms.</p></details>

  <article class="research-item">
    <p class="research-title">Parents' Access to Schools and Intergenerational Human Capital: Evidence from India</p>
    <p class="research-venue">Journal of Human Resources, conditionally accepted, 2025.</p>
    <div class="paper-actions">
      <a class="paper-button" href="/Published%20Papers/2025,%20School%20Construction%20%26%20Intergenerational%20Human%20Capital%20(Conditionally%20Accepted,%20JHR).pdf">Published version</a>
      <details class="abstract-toggle"><summary>Abstract</summary><p>This paper estimates the direct and intergenerational effects of one of the world's largest school expansion policies. Starting in 1993-94, India's District Primary Education Programme constructed over 100,000 new schools and served over 50 million children. Using a fuzzy regression discontinuity design, the paper shows that the policy increased enrollment, literacy, and years of education for direct beneficiaries. Children whose mothers were beneficiaries had higher vernacular, math, and English test scores, while fathers' exposure had no effect on children's learning.</p></details>
    </div>

  <article class="research-item">
    <p class="research-title">Improving Child Learning through Community-School Partnerships</p>
    <p class="research-authors">With Ricardo Sabates, Deepak Kumar, Suman Bhattacharjea, and Wilima Wadhwa.</p>
    <p class="research-venue">Labor Economics, 2024.</p>
    <div class="paper-actions">

      <details class="abstract-toggle"><summary>Abstract</summary><p>Due to almost-universal enrollment in primary schools, policy focus has shifted toward improving learning outcomes. This study examines two accountability strategies: one involving only the community and another building collaboration between schools and communities. A randomized controlled trial in 400 villages in India finds that both interventions significantly enhanced children's foundational literacy and numeracy skills, with the community-school intervention showing stronger effects when parents reported visiting the school.</p></details>

    <p class="research-title">Education and Mental Health: Average and Distributional Effects in the Long Run</p>
    <p class="research-authors">With Yanan Li.</p>
    <p class="research-venue">Labor Economics, 2024.</p>
    <div class="paper-actions">
      <a class="paper-button" href="/Published%20Papers/2024,%20Distributional%20Effects%20of%20Education%20on%20Mental%20Health%20(Labour%20Economics).pdf">Published version</a>
      <details class="abstract-toggle"><summary>Abstract</summary><p>This paper uses exogenous variation induced by China's 1986 compulsory schooling law to study the mean and heterogeneous effects of education on mental health. Regression discontinuity estimates suggest that beneficiaries had better mental health and a lower probability of severe depression. Distributional analysis shows that the average effect is largely driven by improvements in the top half of the mental health distribution, suggesting that looking beyond average effects can better inform targeting.</p></details>

  <article class="research-item">
    <p class="research-title">Health Insurance and Infant Mortality: Evidence from India</p>
    <p class="research-authors">With Anaka Aiyar.</p>
    <p class="research-venue">Health Economics, 2024.</p>
    <div class="paper-actions">
      <a class="paper-button" href="/Published%20Papers/2024,%20Health%20insurance%20and%20child%20mortality%20(Health%20Economics).pdf">Published version</a>
      <details class="abstract-toggle"><summary>Abstract</summary><p>This paper documents the child-mortality-reducing effects of a national health insurance policy in India. The Rashtriya Swasthya Bima Yojana was rolled out between 2008 and 2013. Leveraging temporal and spatial variation in implementation, the analysis finds that the program lowered infant mortality by 6 percent and under-five mortality by 5 percent, with effects concentrated among urban poor households and mechanisms tied to reproductive health service use and complementary care such as immunizations.</p></details>

    <div class="paper-actions">

The results are consistent across definitions of diet diversity and reference periods, pointing to men's education as a pathway for improving nutritional outcomes within households.</p></details>

  <article class="research-item">
    <p class="research-title">Marriage Age, Social Status, and Intergenerational Effects in Uganda</p>
    <p class="research-venue">Demography, 2019.</p>
    <div class="paper-actions">
      <a class="paper-button" href="/Published%20Papers/2019,%20Intergenerational%20Effects%20of%20Marriage%20Age%20(Demography).pdf">Published version</a>
      <details class="abstract-toggle"><summary>Abstract</summary><p>This study examines whether Ugandan women who marry at younger ages fare differently later in life than women who marry later. Using age at menarche as an instrumental variable, the paper finds that a one-year delay in marriage increases educational attainment, literacy, and labor force participation. Children of mothers who marry later have higher BMI and hemoglobin levels and are less likely to be anemic, with mechanisms related to women's agency and assortative matching.</p></details>
